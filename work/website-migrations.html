The problem

We were asked to move WordPress sites onto the company's Cloudways hosting. The requests sounded alike. The jobs were not. One was a full move off old hosting. One only needed the live site switched over. One asked for a transfer, but there was no site yet.

A site move can go wrong quietly. Mail stops when a DNS record is lost. A staging copy sends real emails to real people. Links break when the domain changes inside the database. I wanted a way to do this that I could repeat and check.

What I did

  • My first migration (2025). A sister company's marketing site, from cPanel hosting to Cloudways. My manager wrote the plan and a colleague moved the DNS zone. The DNS records were exported first. I did the hands-on migration and asked Cloudways support for help when it got stuck. It went live in April 2025.
  • A production cutover (2026). A small coaching business's site. This was a cutover, not a full migration. I wrote a 4-step plan, my lead approved it, and I did the Cloudways steps paired with a colleague. The customer changed DNS in their own Cloudflare. Live in July 2026.
  • A clinic group's two sites (2026). The main site moved from an agency's managed host to Cloudways staging. The plugin restore was blocked, so I restored a backup of about 3.9 GB and 48,050 files by hand. I fixed 51 invalid database values and 384 encoded links, and added a staging guard that blocks outgoing mail and form submissions. Much of the server work ran in Codex sessions I directed. I took the group's second site, an orthopaedic one, to staging too. The client withdrew before either went live.
  • Practice runs (2026). During hosting training I did four practice migrations between test servers with a colleague. After that I was named one of the migration engineers for the company's WordPress hosting beta.

Then two builds, with nothing to migrate.

  • A new brand for the clinic group (2026). Its old site-builder page had nothing to export. I rebuilt it as a one-page WordPress site from copy, colours and fonts I checked first. Then I did the DNS cutover. Live in September 2026.
  • A financial-services licensing firm (2026). The request said "transfer", but there was no existing site. I built a new WordPress site with a custom PHP theme rebuilt from the client's HTML. Tailwind is compiled, not loaded from a CDN. The enquiry form has a honeypot, a signed timestamp and a rate limit. The site had a security review before launch and read-only checks after. Live in November 2026.

Last, I turned these jobs into a WordPress migration skill for the team, with audit, migrate and cleanup modes and 156 tests. I replayed it on paper against 5 past jobs. One case failed on the first run, and its expected result was then changed. One lesson is built in: several jobs that arrived as "migrations" turned out not to be. The PR is still open, not merged.

The cutover steps I wrote into the skill

These are the steps the migration skill writes down. Not every job used every step.

  1. Snapshot DNS first

    Export every DNS record before touching anything. If a record goes missing later, there is a copy to put back.

  2. Build on staging

    The new site sits on a password-protected staging copy with noindex, so the public and search engines do not see it.

  3. Get the client's approval

    The client checks staging and says yes before anything goes live.

  4. Change only root and www

    Point the root and www records at the new server. Leave mail and every other record as it was.

  5. Issue SSL

    Get a Let's Encrypt certificate for the new server once the records point at it.

  6. Back up, then purge caches

    Take a backup of the live site, then purge the Breeze and Varnish caches so nobody sees a stale page.

  7. Check without changing anything

    After go-live, run checks that only read. Nothing gets edited while I confirm the site works.

On the first job a colleague moved DNS. On the coaching cutover the customer changed DNS themselves.

What changed

On the new brand site, the cutover touched 2 of the domain's 12 DNS records. The other 10, mail included, were left alone.

Four sites went live: two moves and two new builds. Two migrations stopped at staging because the client withdrew. The 4 practice runs are what led to my place on the hosting beta.

The habit I would keep is changing as little as possible at the moment of cutover. Most of the risk in a move is in the records you did not mean to touch. On the new brand site I exported the records first, changed two, and checked the rest afterwards without editing anything.
